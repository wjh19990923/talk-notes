How 21 People Built a $100M+ Agent Company

A year of hiring constraints pushed Amp to organize work around its own coding agent. Quinn Slack explains how the team ships in small, monitored steps, follows one bug report to other affected customers, and builds internal tools around the work it actually does.

01

The agent that could check its own work

In January 2025, Quinn Slack asked an early agent to change its own background color. The task was tiny. What caught his attention was what the agent did after receiving it. It ran commands, looked at what it had done, and used the result as feedback for its next action.

Slack was CEO of Sourcegraph, a 200-person enterprise software company that built code search. He set aside sales calls and executive meetings to work on the agent. He could reorganize teams and waive requirements such as pull request review, yet changing how the whole company worked proved hard. About 20 people formed the Amp team inside Sourcegraph and tried working differently there.

02

Enterprise checklists and features competitors can copy

The familiar enterprise play was to find early demand, build what enterprise buyers expected, and hire a sales team. Slack thought that order was risky for coding agents. Spending three months satisfying the typical enterprise buyer could leave the product behind as capabilities changed. Amp concentrated on making something people inside those companies wanted to use.

Orbs, Amp’s cloud agents, and multiplayer gave the company features it could name in a pitch. Showing that those features worked also gave competitors a target to build toward. Slack recalls a user who felt Amp’s appeal only made sense once you tried it. If the value takes using the product to recognize, keeping customers close to the people who build it gives those people a better chance of understanding what users are responding to.

03

The spinout had to make do with 21 people

Amp spun out of Sourcegraph with 21 people. Slack says investors and employees got ownership in both companies. The legal spin-off process constrained hiring and option grants for a year, until the Friday before the talk. Amp had to decide what it could get done with the people it had brought over.

Mandatory pull requests, meetings, and enterprise deals that took months all competed for the same people’s time. Amp loosened its development process and gave individuals more room to act. Slack saw each teammate’s stake in the business as part of what made that autonomy workable.

What did the hiring constraint look like next to competitors’ growth? Slack used a log-scale headcount chart to contrast Amp holding at 21 with rivals adding people. On that scale, equal vertical distances represent equal multiples of headcount. There was an exception to the hiring constraint. One person joined despite expecting to wait about eight months for options. For the team as a whole, the constraint pushed Amp to find out how much more it could do with the agent.

04

Shared threads and small production pushes

Amp works without Jira, a task tracker, or a separate support system. The team works in Amp threads, where the conversation about a problem gives the agent context for the fix. If someone gets in over their head, a teammate can join the thread and add what they know. The agent can act on the discussion as it develops.

When one person can push at any time, a larger change can go out in pieces small enough to observe individually. Each kind of check answers a different question.

- Logging before a change. First push instrumentation that shows how the current system behaves. The person and the agent can use those observations to check their assumptions before changing the behavior. - Monitoring after a push. Ship a small increment and have the agent watch it for an hour before the next push. If trouble shows up in that window, there’s a small change to investigate. - Comparing a shadow system. Run the proposed implementation alongside the old one and compare what each computes. Agreement shows that the replacement preserves the result on the cases the two systems actually processed.

05

Checking a UI frame by frame and comparing design variants

Does anything jump while the interface loads? The finished screen can look fine even when an element shifts on the way there. Slack had the agent capture every frame of a load and compare the frames visually. Looking across the sequence lets you locate a shift that would disappear by the final frame. The check covers the load the agent captured.

Amp’s dictation waveform gives the agent a different job. Ask it for several visual treatments in one request, then compare them together. Slack uses nine styles as an example of a request that would ask a lot of a designer’s time and patience. A batch gives the person making the decision several options in one pass and saves a round trip for each individual tweak.

06

One bug report reaches two sets of customers

When a customer reports a bug in Amp, the team uses the agent to help fix the issue. Amp emails the reporter to thank them and gives them free credits. Slack treats the acknowledgment as part of putting things right.

What does Amp need to reach people who hit the bug and kept going? The team searches its logs for the same error and uses the matching records to identify other affected users. Imagine 17 more people encountering the same obscure failure. The search tells Amp whom to contact, and the repair gives Amp something to tell them. Slack’s sample email acknowledges the error, says it’s fixed, and offers $10.

Slack expects people who get that email to be more willing to report the next problem. The same team can then act on what they send, with the agent helping make the fix. For a product he describes as built almost entirely with AI, this is one way Amp finds failures in real use and corrects them without routing every issue through product management, design, and a separate engineering team.

07

Speaking to the agent and letting it use the tools

Slack favors dictation because he can give the agent context quickly by talking through a problem. It takes getting over the awkwardness of speaking aloud, and in a shared office everyone can hear you do it. Slack had just sat near another Amp co-founder who was dictating and found it annoying enough to joke that the practice could bring back remote work.

Amp had also connected the agent to tools it used in production. When the agent uses a tool directly, the team can ask which capabilities it still relies on the full product to provide. A narrow enough job becomes a candidate for an internal tool.

08

A CRM sized to Amp’s own workflow

Amp built its own customer relationship management tool. Slack uses Salesforce to illustrate how small that project could be. Say your team uses 2% of Salesforce’s features. Build that slice, then spend another 2% on the integrations you want with the rest of your stack. The figures illustrate how he would scope the build around one team’s needs.

Owning those integrations lets the team change them as its work changes. The people who know how the workflow should fit together can ask the agent to make the connections. Slack imagines taking the same request to a vendor product manager, who would have to make the case internally and hope it lands on the Q4 roadmap.

That option could change the economics of selling software. Slack’s example is a vendor that can pay a salesperson $100,000 to close a deal because it expects ten years of renewals and a little more spending over time. If enough customers find it practical to build the workflows they need, counting on those renewals gets harder.

09

Everyone talks to customers, and the team meets once a week

Everyone at Amp talks to customers. That gives the person who hears about a problem room to act, whether it calls for a product change or a frank conversation.

- Wire transfers for larger payments. Brady, who had been a sales development rep at Sourcegraph, took on building a wire transfer option for customers whose payments were too large for a credit card. - A direct answer to a prospect. An Amp teammate told a prospective customer that the process sounded likely to bog them both down and waste their time. Slack saw room for that kind of answer when people could judge the deal on its merits without a commission riding on it.

Amp works without product managers and holds one meeting a week. For the rest of the week, the guidance is “follow your energy.” Slack doubts most companies could run that way. He ties Amp’s latitude to ownership. With a substantial stake, each teammate has a reason to pick work that helps the whole business, even when it falls outside their former job. He includes Saturday and Sunday in his illustration of the week because he associates that stake with a willingness to put in more time.

10

Amp’s revenue and Slack’s closing challenge

Slack reports that Amp was profitable and had more than $100 million in annual recurring revenue. He also estimates Amp was 40 to 50 times as efficient as traditionally organized companies on a dollar-in, dollar-out basis. The inputs to that calculation and the comparison companies are unspecified, which limits what another team can infer from the multiplier.

Slack closes with “get out of the cage.” He’d heard from developers whose private channels had become a place to talk about how their companies ought to change. His challenge was to act on the work agents make possible beyond the old job description. Push for a change where you are, join a company willing to let agents shape how it works, or start one.

要点总结

  • Small pushes let a team gather evidence while a change is still in progress. Logging tests assumptions about current behavior, monitoring checks a shipped increment, and a shadow system compares old and new outputs on observed cases.
  • One bug report can help people who never filed one. The log search identifies who else hit the error, and the repair gives the team a resolution to share.
  • When an agent calls a tool directly, the team can scope an internal replacement around the capabilities it uses and the integrations it wants. Amp’s CRM applies that idea to one company’s workflow.
