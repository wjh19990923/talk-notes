从一次调仓到一次客户会议：在 ChatGPT 中构建金融智能体

Stefano Fabbri 和 Oliver Steffmann 以一位财富顾问的会前准备为例，展示了 ChatGPT 如何检索金融证据、可复用的技能在哪里塑造分析，以及一家公司什么时候需要通过 Codex 接入自己的工具。

根据金融团队要做的事来选择界面：用 ChatGPT 探索 Rachel 应该就 Jordan 的装修计划问些什么，用 ChatGPT Work 结合客户记录和带引用的公司研究来准备回顾，用 Codex 创建并测试一个可复用的顾问回顾技能。

01

从顾问的工作出发，配置一个金融工作空间

A wealth advisor has to explain why a client’s portfolio changed and what it would take to bring it back into balance. OpenAI’s Stefano Fabbri and Oliver Steffmann chose that assignment for a two-hour workshop with people from asset managers and investment banks. It gave them a way to test several capabilities together. Could an advisor get the relevant financial data, use a model to understand what happened, and carry the evidence through to a presentation they could use with the client?

Fabbri introduced the pair as members of OpenAI’s customer-facing applied AI team. Much of his work was with financial institutions in New York, helping them put models into production and get value from what they spent on them. Steffmann brought experience in asset management and investment banking, along with a developer’s interest in what a firm could build on the platform. Their colleague Dom was in the room to help participants individually. The split in experience informed the agenda. First, use the product as an advisor would. Then, take the internal developer’s seat and decide what to add.

The first action was to scan a QR code and provide an email address. OpenAI used it to grant access to a dedicated workspace with financial data and unlimited tokens for the session. The organizers also enabled an ultrafast mode for GPT-6.1 Sol and GPT-6 Astra. Fabbri put average generation speed in that mode at about 300 tokens per second. The immediate benefit for participants was shorter waits as they tried prompts and generated material. It was a figure for model output speed, separate from the age of the market data they would retrieve.

Fabbri budgeted about ten minutes after the introduction to get everyone into the workspace. Steffmann would lead two practical sessions, beginning in ChatGPT Work and then moving to Codex. The organizers reserved the final twenty minutes for questions about both the engineering and its use in financial institutions. Participants could ask for help as they went, which mattered because getting onto the workshop site and getting access to the financial product involved separate steps.

02

三种界面与金融产品的三个要素

Fabbri distinguished three interfaces. ChatGPT was the original general-purpose product. ChatGPT Work was the environment aimed at knowledge workers, with agents and plugins available in the course of their work. Codex was oriented toward engineering tasks. A business user could work in Codex, as Steffmann would later point out, but the sequence here was deliberate. An engineer could first see what an advisor could accomplish in ChatGPT Work before deciding that the firm needed more software.

Fabbri described ChatGPT for financial services as an enterprise product built on the same underlying model as ChatGPT. He distinguished three parts of the offering. The first was data access. Some financial sources were built in, and users with access to other providers could query those sources through ChatGPT. The second was the model’s proficiency at financial tasks. Fabbri said OpenAI had trained the model on work such as mergers and acquisitions and equity analysis. Retrieving a filing supplies the evidence. Interpreting it for a valuation draws on that financial training.

The third part was producing artifacts. An analyst may need a spreadsheet that another person can inspect or a presentation suitable for a meeting. The model could use a historical example as a template, carrying its format forward into a new analysis. That makes the old presentation useful even when the underlying numbers have changed. It supplies an example of how the team communicates its work.

Steffmann identified what to look for when using the finance plugin in the web interface. Beyond fetching a figure, it could guide the model in choosing how to chart the information, validating the data, and adding notes about its origin. Those instructions matter at the point where a result moves from a conversation into a deck. An advisor explaining a chart needs to know what its series represents and where the values came from.

03

在公司接入自己的数据之前，金融工作空间提供了什么

The dedicated workspace gave participants access to a product Fabbri said was available to enterprise customers. He named Daloopa, LSEG, PitchBook, and Crunchbase among the providers OpenAI worked with. The available material spanned SEC filings, market data, news, private-company profiles, and earnings transcripts. These sources answer different parts of an analyst’s question. A market series can establish that a price moved. A filing or an earnings transcript gives the analyst material to investigate an explanation for the move.

Fabbri estimated that the market data was delayed by about fifteen minutes. For the meeting-preparation case, that qualifies what an advisor can say about a recent observation. Even a model that produces an answer in seconds is working with a price from the time represented by the source. Fabbri gave the delay as an approximation and did not break it out by provider or instrument.

The integration also changed what the model received. Fabbri said the team had repackaged the datasets into forms the model could consume more readily and had seen a significant improvement in how it used them. Steffmann recognized the engineering work from an earlier effort in asset management. Downloading SEC files was one job. Handling the collection at scale and continuing to bring in new information were recurring jobs. He pointed to agents continually collecting data and to a layer of context that made the resulting material usable at speed. Later questions would get much more specific about what belonged in that context.

04

用投行或股票分析插件作为起始流程

Fabbri described a finance plugin as a library of skills packaged with Model Context Protocol (MCP) connections. The skills give the model procedures it can reuse. The connections give it tools with which to carry out parts of those procedures. OpenAI had worked with investment banks, asset managers, and equity analysts on the contents of the skills. The goal was to give a practitioner a recognizable starting method for an assignment that would otherwise require considerable instruction.

Consider an equity analyst with a recurring assignment. Fabbri suggested starting with a supplied skill, using it as the basis of a scheduled task, and seeing how closely the resulting process matches what the analyst wants to repeat. At that point, the analyst can identify the missing instructions and adapt the procedure to their own requirements and views. Fabbri used an informal 80/20 split to describe the division of effort. Industry knowledge might get much of the base in place, leaving the practitioner to supply the last part. He offered that as a way to think about customization, without claiming a measured completion rate.

The ready-made examples leaned toward investment banking. They included preparing presentations, analyzing earnings, performing valuations, and building leveraged buyout (LBO) models. The day’s exercise would instead follow wealth management, where the immediate question concerned a particular client’s portfolio. One of the presenters recalled having discussed asset valuation with bankers despite already knowing capital markets. Trying the plugin on a few local examples helped him understand more quickly the problem those customers had been describing. Running a recognizable case gave the practitioner something concrete to compare with their own method.

05

跟随变化从客户的投资组合进入会议演示稿

As participants confirmed that they could sign in, the presenters directed them to the workshop site. A QR code led to the development material, with an access code available if the site requested one. They allowed time for people to get in before proceeding. Meanwhile, Steffmann explained what the advisor was supposed to accomplish with the financial data and the supplied guidance.

The starting event was a change in a client’s portfolio. The team preparing the meeting had to examine the portfolio, work out what happened, explain it, and prepare material for the advisor. Steffmann specified a profit-and-loss statement or a portfolio attribution as the analytical output. These give the advisor different ways into the conversation. A profit-and-loss statement establishes the gain or loss. Attribution addresses which parts of the portfolio contributed to the result. A market move takes on meaning for this client through what the client held.

The next output was a presentation explaining the change and what the advisor could discuss with the client to restore balance. The analysis and the deck therefore had to agree. If the advisor wanted to discuss a particular holding, the preceding work had to establish why that holding mattered to the portfolio change. The exercise supplied a scenario and prompts, leaving the advisor’s team room to use its own working method. Steffmann had seen considerable variation in how advisors prepared for meetings.

The business objective was more time for the advisor and client to spend together. That put a useful boundary around the engineering exercise. Portfolio research, explanation, and meeting preparation were all candidates for assistance because they consumed time before the conversation. The deck was intended to equip the advisor to conduct that conversation. At this point the presenters had specified the assignment and its intended outputs. They had yet to show a computed attribution, a particular rebalancing recommendation, or a finished client presentation.

06

在独立的 API 应用和扩展现有工作空间之间做选择

Steffmann asked how many participants were developers, engineers, or otherwise involved in technical development. He estimated about half the room. For them, he recast the case as the next request an internal engineer might receive. Suppose ChatGPT has already landed successfully in the finance department. Business users now want it to work with the firm’s data and to fit the way their own teams operate. What should the engineer actually build?

One option was a conventional application. Obtain an OpenAI API key, build a web interface, use Codex to help implement it, and deploy it within the company. The result would have the familiar software development lifecycle of a company application. The other option started with an observation about the users. Many already had enterprise ChatGPT Work, Codex, or both. The engineer could put the new workflow inside an interface those people were already using. For a team trying to shorten application deployment, the interesting question was how much of its requirement that route could satisfy.

Steffmann separated three kinds of customization. A skill is reusable Markdown that tells the model how to perform a task. It fits a difference in working method, such as the procedure an advisor wants followed during analysis. Connecting the firm’s own data introduces a tool requirement. The engineer must define what the agent can call and configure the MCP connection through which it can make those calls. A plugin packages those capabilities with the relevant skills and can be used on supported web and desktop surfaces.

A plugin extension, recently announced at DevDay, addressed a third requirement. A firm might want part of its workflow to appear differently inside the application. An extension let the developer customize parts of the interface for the use case. Steffmann’s distinction gives the engineer a concrete design question at each step. Does the advisor need better instructions? Does the analysis need an internal tool? Does the person working through it need a particular view? Each answer changes the scope of what has to be built.

07

进入正确的工作空间，打开示例客户资料包

An attendee asked what accounted for the finance product’s performance with transcripts, reports, and market data. How much came from the tools and skills, and how much from changes to the model? The question remained open while the presenters helped people get access. They supplied the workshop access code for anyone missing the full link and asked participants to confirm that it worked. The instructions were on a public website hosted with the Sites feature. Invitations to the financial workspace were arriving separately by email.

The site included a small incentive to work through the exercises. Participants who got in received 100,000 in virtual funds and could earn more by completing steps. The organizers planned to recognize the top three with bottles. Seeing people appear on the site helped the presenters tell that registration was progressing. For the finance exercise itself, the meaningful confirmation was being in the ChatGPT workspace where the participant could see the financial data and the ChatGPT for financial services plugin.

Once there, participants could follow the basic-capabilities instructions and do the setup once. A ZIP file supplied the example client data for the wealth-management case. Steffmann invited people to choose among portfolio work, research, and graphics. Each section could be tried independently. Someone interested in research could explore the financial evidence, while someone interested in charts could concentrate on how a result would be presented. The common client case kept those choices tied to the same advisor’s assignment.

Participants interested chiefly in the financial product could stay in that part of the exercise. Those interested in development could install or open Codex on the desktop and begin its setup. The developer material would cover a skill and plugin, an MCP connection to supplement the workflow, and a plugin extension. Keeping the basic exercise available mattered. An engineer deciding what to add could first try the existing product against the sample client data and see which part of the job already worked.

08

让桌面视图响应对话或文件

Steffmann returned to the structure of a plugin to explain what the extension added. A plugin could contain skills, an MCP server, configuration for the tools, and interface elements for presenting the result. The extension gave the developer control over designated areas of the desktop interface. What appeared there could depend on the conversation a wealth-management user was having with ChatGPT or Codex.

His concrete example was a particular file type. The developer could arrange for an extension-controlled view to appear when that type of file came up in the interaction. The user could expand it and work in an interface tailored to that material. That is a different deliverable from asking the model to produce a chart for a presentation. In one case the analyst needs an artifact to take to a meeting. In the other the analyst needs a way to interact with something while the work is underway. Steffmann described this as a design possibility. He did not identify a specific file format or show a completed client-specific extension in this passage.

09

在设计流水线之前先检查网页、桌面和 CLI 的边界

An access question exposed a consequential constraint. At the time of the workshop, ChatGPT for financial services and its integrated financial data were accessible in the ChatGPT web interface. A company might provide both web ChatGPT and Codex, and a single employee might use both, but the workflow still had to account for which environment could reach the data. An advisor’s successful query on the web established access on that surface.

Plugins were available in ChatGPT Work and on desktop. The interface customization provided by plugin extensions was desktop-only at that point. Codex also had a command-line interface, which made it a natural candidate for a developer building a pipeline. Steffmann specifically cautioned that the CLI could not then retrieve the finance product’s integrated data. This qualified the earlier example of putting a skill on a schedule. Before reproducing a successful interactive analysis in a pipeline, the engineer needs to identify where it will execute and whether the required source is available there.

For the exercise in ChatGPT Work, participants were advised to select Astra or 6.1 Sol, open the speed setting, and choose ultrafast. They could then follow the supplied prompts for historical market data and research. The prompts gave participants a common starting point from which to inspect the product’s behavior and customize it for an advisor’s use case. By the time the presenters checked again, almost everyone had received the email and entered the intended workspace.

10

The evaluation advice has an order. Start with simple checks whose behavior the team understands. Add complexity once it can interpret the results. Beginning with an LLM as judge introduces two uncertain systems at once. When a run gets a poor score, the engineer has to work out whether the agent performed badly or the judge failed to recognize a good result. A simple initial test gives the team a reference point before it makes the evaluation itself depend on model judgment.

When a model judge is warranted, the presenter recommends decomposing a one-to-five rating into individual true-or-false questions. Each question should ask for a yes or a no. In the paper example, an evaluator could ask whether the result includes a numerical comparison with the paper. That is a narrower judgment than rating the entire reconstruction, and a reviewer can inspect the answer that led to it. Separate questions can capture other requirements without asking the judge to decide how much each one contributes to an overall grade.

The presenter mentions the Decision API as one possible implementation of this pattern. He describes it as returning a fast binary decision together with a probability or indication of confidence. The attraction for evaluation is the fit between the interface and the question. An engineer can ask about a specific condition and retain the decision for that condition. Choosing conditions that actually matter to the business still takes the subject-matter work he has just described.

The second half of the audience question concerns automated research. The presenter connects recent gains in mathematics with earlier gains in programming. Quantitative work often needs both. Reproducing a paper requires understanding the mathematical procedure well enough to implement it, then using the implementation to test a scientific claim. He suggests reproduction as an entry point because papers offer tasks at different levels of difficulty and, in cases such as the pricing example, an external result against which to compare the output.

For an iterative problem, he describes Codex goal mode. The user specifies an objective, preferably a numerical one when the problem admits it. His example uses an existing agent, an evaluation dataset and a benchmark. Ask Codex to adjust the agent’s prompt to maximize measured performance. Codex can consider a change, try it, evaluate the result against the target, and keep exploring new ideas and hypotheses. This is what he means by hill climbing. The benchmark gives successive attempts a common measure and supplies a reason to keep or reconsider a change.

That example also explains why the preceding evaluation discussion matters. If a benchmark compresses several requirements into a score the team barely understands, the system can pursue that number while the team learns little about which capability improved. With individual checks available, the researcher has a way to examine the effect of a prompt change. The presenter calls prompt calibration a simple instance of automated research. The same general instruction to pursue a goal can support a much less prescribed investigation.

He points to mathematical results for which the team used goal mode and says that, in one case, it published the prompt. In describing the task he corrects himself from a theorem to a conjecture. The prompt is of interest because it shows how the research objective was put to Codex. Goal mode specifies what to pursue. Ultra influences how readily the agent recruits help pursuing it.

The final audience question picks up a conspicuous feature of the examples. Many skills and plugins read information. Can an agent also write the result back to the system a team works in, such as its accounting system? Dom, the colleague invited to answer, starts by separating orchestration from storage. Codex can coordinate a plugin extension while its tools interact with other systems and store data remotely. The first design decision is where the result needs to exist after the user finishes working on it.

The simplest destination is the project’s local filesystem. A tool can return output in a specified form, and skill instructions can tell Codex how to process it and where in the project to write it. During extended work, the filesystem gives the agent a place to retain intermediate material and return to it. This complements conversation compaction. The harness can manage the size of the conversation while a file holds an output the project still needs. For an individual working locally, that file may itself be the desired result.

For a workflow shared with another application, an extension can use a tool that contacts a central server or connects to a particular database. Dom describes a proxy-voting implementation concerned with what the voting systems had received and where material had been sent. The user worked in Codex. An application server connected through the plugin extension owned the record. The write operation had concrete contents: the report, its findings, the date and the sources. The tool wrote those back to the database that powered a production application outside Codex.

That placement served two audiences. The operator could conduct the work in Codex, while administrators and people responsible for legal verification could use the firm’s existing system of record. Dom describes connecting particular tools through MCP, the Model Context Protocol, and assigning the permissions appropriate to those connections. A builder can keep a project local or expose a path to the service that needs the result. In the proxy-voting case, saving the finished report only in the operator’s workspace would have left the production application without the record it needed.

A second presenter adds one more output for audit-sensitive work. Alongside the artifact, the team often retains standardized logs that let a technical reviewer trace actions and decisions to the data sources and logic behind them. A report gives the reviewer the findings. The log lets that person examine how the workflow reached a particular finding and which action followed. For the financial processes the team describes, retaining that history is part of the plugin’s work when it creates the artifact.
