1,000 Claude Agents Formalized Fermat’s Last Theorem

Anthropic gave Claude 999 helpers and a database. The lead used it to divide a proof into pieces small enough for the team to finish. In a Pokémon game, a single bad entry in Claude’s own notes was enough to send it through the same trapdoor again and again. Lance Martin opens up both runs to show what the agents wrote down and what happened when someone relied on it.

01

The seven-year proof Claude had to put into Lean

Fermat’s Last Theorem fits on one line. For an integer exponent $$n > 2$$, the equation $$a^n + b^n = c^n$$ has no solution in positive integers $$a$$, $$b$$ and $$c$$. Andrew Wiles spent seven years working on the theorem. When his proof appeared in the mid-1990s, someone else could read the argument. To have a computer check it, someone still had to express each step in a language precise enough for a proof checker.

Anthropic reported a formalization in Lean in September. About a dozen Claude agents worked on it, with considerable help from a researcher and a specialized platform for managing proofs. The platform gave the agents a graph they could query to find out how the project was going.

Lance Martin wanted to take that platform away. Lean would still check the mathematics. Who would keep track of the mathematicians?

02

A thousand sandboxes shared just two services

The prompt gave one agent the lead, assigned the other 999 as helpers and supplied the problem statement. Each agent had its own sandbox with restricted network access. All thousand could reach a Git repository and a SQL database. The team got to decide what to put in them and how to divide up the proof.

Leaving the mathematical strategy to Claude was a deliberate choice. Anthropic still monitored every tool call for behavior such as an attempted sandbox escape. A second, offline check reviewed all the agents’ runs every ten minutes.

03

The lead drew up its own tables in five minutes

About five minutes in, the lead started building a project tracker in SQL. “We didn’t tell them to build these tables,” Martin says. The schema is a useful account of what a thousand agents needed to know about one another:

- Who has the work? An assignments table connected helpers to statements in the proof. The lead had an announcements table for news that concerned everyone.

- What does the next worker need to know? Agents left progress notes and broke statements down into subtasks.

- Which work has landed? A separate table tracked contributions that had made it into Lean code. A promising note and a landed contribution had different places to go.

The database trace shows writes in red and reads in green. Notice how rarely the lead writes to the announcements table. Most updates had a smaller audience: the helpers working on a particular statement.

04

Where did the workers go when a statement was proved?

On a hard statement, the helpers devised a task tree of their own. A branch showed the smaller results a larger result depended on. As those pieces were proved, they turned red. That told workers which branches they could pick up next.

Now zoom out to the whole proof. In the allocation view, 44 of the 60 statements are already proved at hour 7.8. The remaining work drew helpers from elsewhere. Sometimes the lead reassigned them; sometimes agents moved themselves. About 30 hours in, the work was finished. One agent called a freeze on the Git origin and signed off with “magnificent work all 999 of you.”

The interesting comparison with September is who did the organizing. The earlier team relied on a purpose-built graph and a researcher. This team built the tracker and adjusted assignments as it went. With all three things changing at once, we can’t infer how much time the larger team saved.

By hour 7.8, Claude agents had proved 44 of 60 statements in the Fermat formalization. The lead and helpers shifted assignments as the proof progressed. Helpers clustered around level lowering and modularity lifting, while 330 of the 999 helpers were idle.

05

The next reader might be a fresh copy of you

A proof worker had the luxury of knowing roughly who would read its note and why. An agent saving a memory for tomorrow has to guess. An observation may seem indispensable halfway through a task, yet mean very little to the next session. Or the agent may leave out the one detail its successor will spend an hour rediscovering.

Martin draws on an idea from sleep research: experience recorded during the day can be revisited and consolidated later. That suggests two occasions to write. One is while working, when the observations are fresh. The other is afterward, when there is enough history to decide which observations belong together. Anthropic tested the first ability by giving Claude an empty directory.

06

Forty questions, forty fresh sessions, one directory

The open-source CLB benchmark asks a sequence of 40 questions about a database. Each question starts a fresh session. In one condition Claude could leave notes for itself in a shared directory. Martin sums up the instruction: “Use memory if you want.” No prompt singled out a fact to save or told the next session when to look it up.

Having the directory barely helped Sonnet 4.6. Its solid bar (memory available) and hatched bar (no memory) sit close together in the results. Anthropic traced that small difference back to two decisions:

- When a session learned something useful, did it leave a note? Sonnet did so inconsistently. Stronger models recorded findings they had checked against the database, sometimes including a prediction another session could test.

- When a new question needed that finding, did the next session look for it? Sonnet seldom thought to read the directory on its own. Stronger models retrieved what they had saved more reliably.

Those questions make a useful debugging sequence. If the first session left the right fact and the second never opened the file, polishing the note won’t fix the missed lookup.

07

In Pokémon, what earns a place in the notebook?

While playing Pokémon, Claude had the same freedom to write to a directory. The boxes in Martin’s side-by-side view are sized by file count. Sonnet 3.7’s boxes are huge. Open the files and much of what it recorded is simply what happened while it played.

Fable 5 kept a smaller notebook of tactics: things a returning player would have trouble deducing from the game screen. It also got further in the game. File count alone can’t explain that difference across two models, but reading the files reveals what the next player inherits. A record of having visited a place is cheap to write. Directions worth following require a better judgment.

08

Dreaming gives the agent a chance to edit its own notes

A transcript preserves details the player never thought to save. Looking for one useful move can mean searching the whole game. The notes are shorter, but they carry the previous player’s interpretation along with its observations. If the interpretation was wrong, a concise note makes the mistake unusually easy to retrieve.

Martin calls a later pass over the transcripts and notes dreaming. The diagram shows two ways that pass can change a future session. Claude can build a wiki page and leave it for the next session to consult. It can also select a detail to load straight into the next session’s context. The latter saves a lookup. It also gives a mistaken detail a head start on the evidence the new player is about to see.

What does a future session look up or receive automatically? A later session can search transcripts and notes directly. Those same records can feed a dreaming pass. A wiki gives the session organized knowledge to consult, while memories selected for injection arrive at startup. - Transcripts — Searchable records of earlier tasks. - Memory notes — Observations the agent chose to save during a task. - Dreaming pass — Revisits existing records and reorganizes selected information. - Wiki — Organized knowledge to consult for later questions. - Injected context — Selected memories loaded into every future session. - Future session — Can receive selected memories at startup and retrieve more information as needed. - Transcripts → Dreaming pass (Records for review) - Memory notes → Dreaming pass (Observations for review) - Transcripts → Future session (Details found through search) - Memory notes → Future session (Notes retrieved on demand) - Dreaming pass → Wiki (Organize knowledge into pages) - Dreaming pass → Injected context (Select memories to preload) - Wiki → Future session (Pages consulted as needed) - Injected context → Future session (Load at session start)

09

The landmark was in the wrong place. The trapdoor wasn’t.

During one Pokémon run, Claude wrote down the wrong location for a landmark. On its next visit it used that note to work out where it was, made a move and fell through a trapdoor to the previous level. It found its way back. The note was still there. Once again Claude placed itself in the wrong spot and lost the ground it had just regained.

Watch the trajectory at that point. Up means the player has made progress; down means it has lost some. The run using raw memory keeps dropping back. The orange run, which includes a dreaming pass, clears the trap and reaches the next level. The chart doesn’t show the text of the revised memory. It shows something a neater notebook couldn’t establish on its own: on returning to the problem, the agent got past it.

10

A wiki Claude could build before it saw the questions

After Anthropic acquired Stainless, the team anonymized a collection of its documents and Slack messages. They wrote 25 questions whose answers required information from that collection. Semantic search alone scored about 53%. What if Claude got time with the files before anyone asked a question?

Anthropic spent that extra compute having Claude build a wiki from the records. With a larger token budget for preparation, the wiki let Claude answer more questions. Giving it both the wiki and a search tool also beat search alone in the comparison. A page could bring facts from several records together, while search kept the original material within reach for questions the wiki author hadn’t anticipated.

11

Follow the links the first draft left empty

Martin closes with a second wiki from a different benchmark. Claude had created placeholders for pages it intended to write. A reader who followed one of those links arrived at an empty page. The first draft was incomplete, but it contained a remarkably precise list of its own omissions.

In the second version, a dreaming pass has filled those pages in. That gives a team trying this on its own documents a modest first experiment. Pick a question that led a colleague to an empty page. Give the agent another pass over the records, then follow the same link. Can the colleague answer the question now?

要点总结

  • The most revealing artifact in the 1,000-agent run is a SQL schema nobody asked for. Five minutes in, the lead had a way to distinguish a helper’s progress note from a piece of Lean code that had actually landed.
  • When memory gains look flat, inspect two sessions. Did the first one leave a finding worth saving? Did the next one open that file when it needed the answer? In the database benchmark Sonnet 4.6 struggled with both.
  • In Pokémon, Claude remembered a landmark so faithfully that it kept falling through the same trapdoor. The useful test of a dreaming pass was what happened when the player came back: this time it reached the next level.
