Stop Building Software Factories

A software factory can spend the weekend making an existing product faster. On Monday, how will the team know what to build next? Justin McCarthy likes to put engineers in charge of a sparkling-water company for a minute. You could put caffeine in a can of Spindrift. You could also put its name on motor oil. Only one of those ideas is likely to survive the meeting.

01

When does a software factory earn its keep?

[Justin McCarthy](/speakers/justin-mccarthy) makes his living building software factories at [Diffusion](/orgs/diffusion). With Claude 4, his team had to hand-wire one agent run to the next. Sometimes the output of one round gave the next round enough feedback to converge on something useful. It took a large token bill and, in his words, a “real Rube Goldberg contraption.”

Then the models improved and pieces of the contraption could come off. This left him with an awkward sales pitch. If you already have customers, he wants you to have a factory. If you’re still arguing about what the product should be, a factory lets you spend the entire weekend implementing the losing side of the argument.

02

The support ticket is part of the specification

A customer renews. They keep using a particular feature. One morning it breaks, and they open a ticket describing the job they can no longer do. An engineer about to rewrite that feature has just learned something a clean-room implementation could easily miss. The customer has named the behavior the replacement owes them.

That’s what makes a brownfield product such good material for a factory. Even a rewrite as ambitious as replacing the internals of Bun has a reference point. Run the work customers already depend on and see whether it still succeeds.

03

What must a faster version still do?

At Diffusion, McCarthy calls each commitment a promise. Suppose an agent finds a faster way to run a customer’s workflow. Before handing that result to the next agent, the team has several things to check.

- Behavior. Can the customer still finish the job they bought the product for? - Economics. How much did the faster version cost to run? Can the company still deliver it at the price the customer agreed to? - Security. Does the change preserve the protections the customer relied on?

His slide draws an amplifier. Follow the line from its output back to its input. Cut it and the circuit can amplify noise. A loop that feeds an agent only its last patch has the same problem. Give it the results of those checks and it has something to act on.

04

Maintenance can run after the team goes home

A slow path on Friday evening is still slow on Saturday morning. Once the team has described the behavior it needs to keep, an agent can keep trying fixes while the office is empty. McCarthy imagines a commit graph gradually losing its nights and weekends.

What interests him is the human calendar. The agents can still be attending to last week’s promises when the team goes out on Monday to ask a customer what they need next.

05

After a model upgrade, see which machinery you can remove

At Diffusion, the written instructions for individual agent tasks got shorter. The harnesses that ran those tasks grew general enough to serve more of them. Some of the work the team had painstakingly encoded in its first factory was now work the underlying model could do.

McCarthy puts up two generations of the Raptor rocket engine. The first had a tangle of tubes and it flew. The second fit roughly the same space, with fewer moving parts and higher performance. Rocket engineers had to design that simplification themselves. A software team can inherit some of it with the next model or harness release. If a loop barely works today, McCarthy would keep it around long enough to find out which pieces the next release lets him throw away.

06

“The same but faster” gives an agent a finish line

The useful word in McCarthy’s instruction is same. A faster feature is an easy win when its behavior, cost and security still meet expectations. If the speedup drives up the bill, someone has to decide what the extra speed is worth.

“Better” asks a different question. Choosing what the experience should feel like takes taste. Choosing what a customer will value may send the team back into discovery. McCarthy estimates that standing up a first factory around existing commitments takes him about a week. He’d spend that week on work with a recognizable finish line, then let the loop keep running.

07

When code is plentiful, which experiment deserves a customer’s time?

McCarthy surveys the code pouring out of these systems. “Even worse, it’s working.” If the customer asked for higher margins and got another screenful of buttons, passing the tests will be small comfort.

His proposed demand factory has an input that is getting harder to come by. It needs somebody willing to talk to you. Say you have a chance to ask a buyer about price tomorrow. An agent could read yesterday’s replies, compare them with earlier conversations and surface the question you haven’t answered. You might discover that the buyer has already told you what they would pay. Spend the call learning what would get them to switch. This is a proposal McCarthy wants engineers to try.

08

Find your company’s energy drink

The Spindrift thought experiment gives his proposal a limit. A buyer might trust a sparkling-water company to make a caffeinated drink. Trust with a car engine is a bigger ask. McCarthy calls that territory the brand envelope. His instruction is to “figure out what your energy drink is.”

Look at the two directions on his slide. Products move toward customers. Information about what they would buy has to travel back. A team that keeps increasing the first flow can exhaust the same group of buyers. To sell them something else, it has to learn what other job they’d hire the company to do.

McCarthy’s closing slide points engineers to the machinery for the brownfield work. The other half of the job starts with an ordinary customer conversation. Pick the adjacent product your team keeps proposing. Ask a buyer what they use for that job today. Would they trust you to replace it?

Products flow from the software factory to the demand factory, while high-volume, high-quality signals flow back. McCarthy highlights how to win a larger share of each customer’s spending by selling more SKUs and grouping them into product lines.

要点总结

  • Before a rewrite, find the behavior customers keep paying for. Usage shows which paths matter. The support ticket when one breaks often tells you exactly what the replacement has to preserve.
  • “The same but faster” is a useful assignment for an agent. A speedup that keeps the feature, cost and security promises can go back into the loop. A speedup that raises the bill needs a human judgment.
  • Customer experiments get scarce as code gets cheap. McCarthy would put agents to work comparing what customers said yesterday with what they have said before, so the next experiment answers a question worth their time.
