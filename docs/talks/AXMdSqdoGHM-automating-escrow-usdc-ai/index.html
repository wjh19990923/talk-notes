用 USDC 和 AI 实现托管交易自动化

跟随一个托管交易工作流，从协议信息抽取到注资智能合约再到图片审核，然后看看哪些环节仍然需要人工审批。

对 API、区块链钱包和智能合约有基本了解会有帮助；文中会讲解托管工作流用到的 USDC 操作。

01

在资金流动之前必须发生什么？

An escrow agreement connects two different problems: deciding whether someone has fulfilled an obligation, and releasing the money once they have. AI can help inspect the evidence; a smart contract can enforce the permitted movement of funds. Combining those capabilities is the starting point for Corey Cooper, who introduces himself as Circle’s DevRel lead.

Cooper describes USDC as an order of magnitude better for escrow than traditional payment rails, but supplies no benchmark for that comparison. His concrete premise is that AI can verify a workflow’s conditions before a programmable dollar settles the resulting payment. He describes that settlement as instant and faster than T+2 globally. The walkthrough moves from Circle’s infrastructure to the USDC contract interface, then to a recently open-sourced escrow application offered for local experimentation.

02

发行方、流动性服务和开发者工具

Circle supplies the dollar infrastructure beneath the application. Cooper describes a fintech company founded in 2013, with BlackRock and Fidelity among its backers, issuing tokenized dollars and euros backed one-to-one by reserves. He characterizes those reserves as fiat and short-term treasuries, and presents stablecoin regulation as a way to make the technology more usable for businesses and individuals.

Cooper reports more than $26 trillion in cumulative onchain transaction volume and roughly 20 supported blockchains at the time of the talk. Those figures describe the broader payment network, not escrow-specific volume or application throughput. The product map extends beyond USDC and EURC: following Circle’s Hashnote acquisition, Cooper describes a tokenized money market fund and the prospect of round-the-clock conversion into USDC. Institutional liquidity services handle minting and redemption into enterprise vault wallets for payments, dollar access and capital markets.

The developer-facing services divide the integration work into distinct responsibilities:

| Service | Responsibility | |---|---| | Circle Wallets | Embed and provision wallets programmatically | | Circle Contracts | Deploy contracts and read or write their state | | Circle Paymaster | Pay gas from a USDC balance | | Cross-Chain Transfer Protocol, or CCTP | Move USDC between blockchain networks |

Paymaster addresses the need to hold a chain’s native token merely to transact. CCTP addresses a different problem: a user’s USDC may live on a network other than the one an application uses. Cooper describes permissionless transfers between supported networks and previews further balance-unification work without specifying its implementation. These are the services as presented in the recording; the walkthrough does not substitute later products or APIs.

03

银行美元如何变成可编程美元

USDC’s ledger lives on blockchain networks, with smart contracts exposing operations that applications can call. Cooper describes global transfers in seconds and uses email and text messaging as the analogy: public networks let developers build sending, receiving and storage experiences across borders. Open participation in that network is distinct from the approval process for a business minting directly with Circle.

The issuance flow connects those public networks to bank-held reserves:

1. A business receives Circle’s approval and opens a Circle Mint account. 2. It connects its bank account and wires fiat into Circle’s reserve bank account. 3. After the USD settles, Circle mints the corresponding USDC and credits the business’s Circle Mint account. 4. To exit, the business redeems USDC through the reverse process at one dollar per USDC.

Cooper points to Circle’s transparency page and monthly attestations as ways to inspect reserve backing, alongside independent oversight. The distinction matters for the escrow application: an onchain transfer and the bank-funded issuance or redemption process are separate operations.

04

支出权限、共同审批和离线准备

Before deploying USDC on another blockchain, Circle looks for standardized behavior and protective controls. Cooper begins with allow lists and block lists: sanctioned or malicious addresses can be prevented from transacting, including with other users. Public access to the network therefore coexists with issuer controls over the token.

Delegated spending lets one party spend an authorized amount from another wallet’s balance. Cooper’s example is a corporate USDC wallet backing employee virtual debit cards, with a workflow imposing spending thresholds or daily caps. The token’s spending authorization supplies a building block; the business workflow determines the policy around it. Cooper says this delegation could scale to tens of thousands of cards or users, without presenting a load test.

Other controls address who may create money and who may authorize its movement:

- Minter configuration: the token contract can authorize additional minting entities. Cooper suggests financial institutions with assigned minting balances as a future possibility, while saying Circle had not implemented that arrangement. - Multisignature approval: a payment workflow can require several parties—such as a CEO, CFO and COO—to approve transactions above a threshold. This reduces the risk of one person having sufficient authority to drain a wallet.

Offline preparation separates signing from broadcasting. For scheduled payroll, a company can prepare a transaction with a known amount, obtain approval and signatures offline, and broadcast it on the payment date. The audience presses on whether that also proves the money will be available. Cooper’s answer is no: preparing a signed transaction does not itself reserve funds or prevent the later payment from failing. Proof-of-funds behavior would require additional design.

05

应用调用的合约接口

Cooper next discusses pausing and upgrading the contract, using USDC v2.2 as the example of improving gas efficiency. The historical release was announced in November 2023 and rolled out across supported EVM deployments; its changes should not be read as an identical upgrade to every blockchain implementation.

The core functions form a small interface for inspecting balances and authorizing or moving tokens:

| Function | Purpose | |---|---| | balanceOf | Read an address’s token balance | | totalSupply | Read the token’s total supply | | allowance | Read an owner’s authorization for a spender | | transfer | Send tokens from the caller | | transferFrom | Move tokens using spending authorization | | approve | Set a spender’s allowance |

In Solidity, that interface can be expressed directly:

```solidity pragma solidity ^0.8.20;

interface IUSDCToken { function balanceOf(address account) external view returns (uint256); function totalSupply() external view returns (uint256); function allowance(address owner, address spender) external view returns (uint256); function transfer(address to, uint256 amount) external returns (bool); function transferFrom(address from, address to, uint256 amount) external returns (bool); function approve(address spender, uint256 amount) external returns (bool); } ```

Cooper focuses on transferFrom: its arguments identify the source address, destination address and amount. An application submits a transaction invoking that operation, and the blockchain processes the resulting token movement. The source address is an argument, not a substitute for spending authorization. This is the programmable interface the later escrow flow builds around.

06

从智能体支付到一个托管交易应用

Agents introduce a reason to care about settlement and verification together. Cooper argues that near-instant payment confirmation suits software transacting with other software, while chargebacks on reversible rails become difficult to manage as automated money movement accelerates. Public blockchain infrastructure also supports transactions outside banking hours, with signatures and publicly inspectable transactions providing cryptographic verification.

Conditional payment logic belongs in a contract the agent can interact with. An agent may need to establish that some prerequisite has been satisfied before requesting a transfer. Composable smart contracts supply the rules governing that transfer; the agent supplies an interaction with those rules. Escrow makes the separation concrete because evidence of completed work must precede release of the funds.

The sample application combines three building blocks. Circle Wallets provisions wallets for the agent and the two escrow parties. A Solidity escrow template is deployed repeatedly through Circle Contracts for different agreements. USDC supplies the currency moving through those contracts, with fiat on-ramping before funding and a possible fiat off-ramp for the beneficiary afterward. The application reuses a contract design; it does not ask the model to invent a new financial program for every agreement.

The business and freelancer first create accounts on the platform. Behind each signup, an API call provisions a wallet. The business then uploads a PDF agreement negotiated outside the application. An OpenAI multimodal model extracts the payment amount and the tasks the freelancer must complete.

Extraction produces a proposed listing, not an immediately binding payment instruction. The business reviews the amount, task list and agreement summary before publishing. Once approved, those values feed a prewritten contract template. Its configuration identifies USDC as the currency, the depositor wallet, the beneficiary wallet and an agent wallet whose described authority is limited to releasing funds. The model supplies agreement data; the template supplies the executable rules.

Deployment is another explicit operation. The platform calls Circle’s APIs and reports success after the contract is deployed. Developers can inspect the activity in Circle’s console and use webhooks to build notifications or other workflows around indexed onchain events. This creates a boundary between requesting an action and observing its completion—a distinction that becomes visible during the live demonstration.

谁应该做最终的付款决定？

An audience member challenges the central automation step: LLMs can be unreliable and nondeterministic, so how far should a payment system minimize human intervention? Cooper distinguishes Circle’s focus on frictionless settlement from the uncertainty introduced by an agent’s judgment. AI is nondeterministic; payment execution is something businesses want to be deterministic.

His recommendation is human approval supported by agent review. An agent can work continuously, assemble a list of payments it considers ready, and explain the evidence behind each recommendation. A payment-operations professional remains the final approver. This is a more conservative operating model than automatically paying whenever an image evaluator returns high confidence. Cooper regards full autonomous control of sending and receiving payments as premature, offers a speculative five-to-ten-year horizon for greater trust, and invites developers to experiment and provide feedback.

Asked for real-world autonomous work-evaluation and settlement examples, Cooper says Circle has not released a production system doing this. A question about zero-knowledge proofs introduces another way to strengthen evidence. Cooper points to TLSNotary communications as relatively lightweight to prove and suggests passing a proof into an escrow workflow. He cites zkP2P as a working experiment, without giving implementation details or proof-generation measurements. TLSNotary’s provenance and selective disclosure of TLS data address whether evidence came from a particular source; deciding whether that evidence satisfies a freelancer’s agreement is still a separate question.
